# Dispositions and Subsequent History

What a disposition did to the *opinion*, jurisdiction by jurisdiction. Load this when you have a disposition, notation, or subsequent-history flag in front of you and need to know whether the opinion below is still citable.

---

## Decision procedure

Run these in order. Do not conclude anything until you reach the end.

1. **What was operated on — the judgment or the opinion?** They are separable. Many orders vacate a judgment and say nothing about the opinion. In the federal system vacating the judgment ordinarily carries the opinion with it; in Texas, New Mexico, Wisconsin, Nevada, Michigan (in part), Hawaii, and elsewhere it does not. Never infer opinion-vacatur from judgment-vacatur without checking the row.

2. **Read the order's own words.** Most jurisdictions let the higher court vacate the opinion expressly. An order that says so controls over any default. An order that is silent falls back to the row. (Texas: Tex. R. App. P. 56.3 makes silence mean the opinion *survives*; the Supreme Court of Texas has nonetheless vacated opinions expressly, twice — see the Texas note.)

3. **Was the disposition on the merits?** Vacatur to effectuate a settlement, vacatur for mootness, GVR, dismissal for want of jurisdiction, and vacatur to permit reconsideration are not merits rulings. They may end the opinion's precedential force without saying anything about whether it was right.

4. **Did the later court have reviewing authority over the earlier one?** A coordinate court's disagreement is a split, not adverse treatment. In many states one intermediate-court panel or district cannot overrule another at all (Ill., N.Y., La., Fla., Ohio, Pa., Mich., Ga., Ind., Wis., and the federal circuits' prior-panel rules).

5. **Was the earlier decision precedential to begin with?** A non-precedential disposition cannot be "still good law" in the binding sense and usually cannot supply adverse treatment either. Check the unpublished column *before* concluding a case was overruled by an unpublished decision.

6. **Check the date.** A dozen jurisdictions changed the rule on a hard date. Getting the date wrong flips the answer: Cal. (July 1, 2016), Ga. (Aug. 1, 2020), Ark. (July 1, 2009), Ill. (Jan. 1, 2021), Ind. (Jan. 1, 2023), Md. (July 1, 2023), Mass. (Feb. 26, 2008), Minn. (Aug. 1, 2020), Nev. (Jan. 1, 2016 / Aug. 15, 2024), Pa. (May 1, 2019 / Jan. 15, 2008), Wash. (Mar. 1, 2013), Wis. (July 1, 2009), Haw. (July 1, 2008), 5th Cir. (Jan. 1, 1996), D.C. Cir. (Jan. 1, 2002), Utah (Oct. 1, 1998–Dec. 31, 2010), FRAP 32.1 (Jan. 1, 2007), Tex. (Sept. 1, 1997; Jan. 1, 2003), 11th Cir. (Oct. 1, 1981), Fed. Cir. (Sept. 30, 1982), D.C. (Feb. 1, 1971), Mich. (Nov. 1, 1990).

7. **Only then name the disposition** using the taxonomy below, and report what it did *and did not* do.

## Taxonomy

### Does NOT end the opinion's precedential force

| Disposition | Why | Anchor |
|---|---|---|
| Reversed / vacated **on other grounds** | Only the point reached is displaced | *Durning v. Citibank, N.A.*, 950 F.2d 1419, 1424 n.2 (9th Cir. 1991): "A decision may be reversed on other grounds, but a decision that has been vacated has no precedential authority whatsoever." |
| Abrogated on other grounds | Displacement runs only as far as the intervening authority reaches; must be "clearly on point" (11th Cir.) or "clearly irreconcilable" (9th Cir.) | *United States v. Archer*, 531 F.3d 1347, 1352 (11th Cir. 2008); *Miller v. Gammie*, 335 F.3d 889, 893, 900 (9th Cir. 2003) (en banc) |
| Affirmed on other grounds; reformed and affirmed | Judgment stands; opinion undisturbed | — |
| Superseded by statute | Statutory holding displaced prospectively; the rest survives | *United States v. Chhien*, 266 F.3d 1, 11 (1st Cir. 2001); *Jacobs v. Nat'l Drug Intelligence Ctr.*, 548 F.3d 375, 378 (5th Cir. 2008) |
| Cert. denied / review denied / allocatur denied / certif. denied / PDR refused | Signifies nothing about the merits, in every jurisdiction checked | *United States v. Carver*, 260 U.S. 482, 490 (1923); *Maryland v. Baltimore Radio Show*, 338 U.S. 912, 919 (1950) (Frankfurter, J.) |
| Cert. / review **granted**, pending | Opinion generally remains binding until the higher court rules — expressly so in Mich., N.M., Conn., Pa., Wis., Nev.; **not** in Ind., Kan., Ky., Ark., Okla. | Mich. Ct. R. 7.215(C)(2); Rule 12-405(C) NMRA; *Collins v. Dep't of the Treasury*, 83 F.4th 970 (5th Cir. 2023) |
| Dismissed as improvidently granted (DIG) | Leaves the decision below exactly as it stood | *Commonwealth v. Tilghman*, 673 A.2d 898, 904 (Pa. 1996); *Hollins v. Wal-Mart Stores*, 709 S.E.2d 625, 625–26 (S.C. 2011) |
| Vacated to effectuate a settlement (Texas) | The opinion survives by rule | Tex. R. App. P. 56.3 |
| Rehearing denied / overruled | The ordinary step that finalizes the disposition | — |
| Amended opinion | The amended text supersedes; untouched holdings persist. **Cite the amended version.** | Fed. R. App. P. 40(d)(1), (f) |

### DOES end it

| Disposition | Why | Anchor |
|---|---|---|
| Reversed or overruled on the merits by a court with reviewing authority | The holding is displaced | — |
| Vacated (federal default) | "Of necessity our decision vacating the judgment of the Court of Appeals deprives that court's opinion of precedential effect" | *O'Connor v. Donaldson*, 422 U.S. 563, 577 n.12 (1975); *County of Los Angeles v. Davis*, 440 U.S. 625, 634 n.6 (1979) |
| *Munsingwear* vacatur (moot pending review) | Prevents "a judgment, unreviewable because of mootness, from spawning any legal consequences" | *United States v. Munsingwear, Inc.*, 340 U.S. 36, 39–41 (1950) |
| GVR | Vacates the judgment — **but decides nothing on the merits** | *Lawrence v. Chater*, 516 U.S. 163, 167–68 (1996) |
| Express vacatur of the opinion itself | Controls over any default | — |
| Withdrawn and superseded on rehearing | The withdrawn text is gone | Tex. R. App. P. 19; *Truddle v. Baptist Mem'l Hosp.-DeSoto*, 150 So. 3d 692, 696 (Miss. 2014) |
| Depublication (Cal., Ariz., Haw.) | Opinion loses precedential force, and in Cal./Ariz. loses citability | Cal. R. Ct. 8.1125; Ariz. R. Civ. App. P. 28(e); *State v. Curtis*, 137 Haw. 43, 51 (App. 2015) |

### Effect is jurisdiction-dependent — always check the row

Grant of discretionary review · grant of rehearing en banc · transfer · stay of the mandate · settlement vacatur · summary/memorandum disposition · unpublished status.

## Texas — long form

### The core rule

Texas separates the judgment from the opinion, and says so twice over.

**Tex. R. App. P. 56.3** (Settled Cases): "The Supreme Court's action may include setting aside the judgment of the court of appeals or the trial court without regard to the merits and remanding the case to the trial court for rendition of a judgment in accordance with the agreement… **In any event, the Supreme Court's order does not vacate the court of appeals' opinion unless the order specifically provides otherwise. An agreement or motion cannot be conditioned on vacating the court of appeals' opinion.**"

***Morath v. Lewis***, 601 S.W.3d 785, 790 (Tex. 2020) (per curiam): "Unlike in federal practice, where vacatur of the appellate judgment entails vacatur of the written judicial opinion supporting the judgment, Texas practice contemplates that a court of appeals' judgment may be vacated without also vacating the corresponding opinion."

***Houston Cable TV, Inc. v. Inwood West Civic Ass'n***, 860 S.W.2d 72, 73 (Tex. 1993) (per curiam): "A settlement does not automatically require the vacating of a court of appeals' opinion — either by this court or by the intermediate appellate court. Our courts are endowed with a public purpose — they do not sit merely as private tribunals to resolve private disputes. While settlement is to be encouraged, a private agreement between litigants should not operate to vacate a court's writing on matters of public importance." And at 73 n.3: "Although the application for writ of error is granted, **the precedential authority of a court of appeals opinion which is not vacated under these circumstances is equivalent to a 'writ dismissed' case.**"

### Two qualifications that matter

**One.** The default is a *default*, not a prohibition. The Supreme Court of Texas has the power to vacate a court of appeals opinion and has exercised it. *Morath* itself granted the State's request to vacate the opinion below in a moot case, reasoning that Rule 60.6's authority to issue "any other appropriate order" reaches opinion-vacatur, and that Rule 56.3 "simply provides a default presumption against vacatur of the opinion for cases that have been mooted by settlement." 601 S.W.3d at 789–91. **Always read the order.**

**Two.** It is very rare. Justice Sullivan's concurrence in *Megatel C90-2, Inc. v. Bank of Utah*, No. 24-0206 (Tex. May 16, 2025) (Sullivan, J., joined by Busby, J., concurring), catalogues the whole list: "We appear to have done so just twice, at most" — *Brazos River Authority v. City of Houston*, No. 21-0642, 2022 WL 4099236, at \*1 (Tex. Sept. 2, 2022) (opinion vacated "[b]ecause the State was not a party to the settlement and the issues presented may be of consequence in other contexts"), and *Farley v. Miranda*, No. 22-0113, 2023 WL 2358183, at \*1 (Tex. Jan. 13, 2023) (remanding to the court of appeals to consider withdrawing its own opinion). *Megatel* denied four such motions at once and reaffirmed *Morath*'s framing that vacatur is a "discretionary equitable remedy" granted only after "carefully scrutiniz[ing] parties' attempts to manipulate judicial precedent by settlement." *Morath*, 601 S.W.3d at 791 (citing *Bonner Mall*, 513 U.S. at 27–28).

So: a Texas court of appeals opinion whose judgment was set aside to effectuate a settlement is **still citable precedent**, at "writ dismissed" weight, unless the order expressly says otherwise.

The vehicle changed on **September 1, 1997**, when both high courts adopted revised rules (*Final Approval of Revisions to the Texas Rules of Appellate Procedure*, 60 Tex. B.J. 876 (Tex. & Tex. Crim. Kenney Mfg. Co.*, 864 F.2d 757, 765 (1988): binding until overturned in banc; "Where there is direct conflict, the precedential decision is the first" |

### Two adoption conventions

**Eleventh Circuit.** *Bonner v. City of Prichard*, 661 F.2d 1206, 1207 (11th Cir. 1981) (en banc): "the decisions of the United States Court of Appeals for the Fifth Circuit…as that court existed on September 30, 1981, handed down by that court prior to the close of business on that date, shall be binding as precedent in the Eleventh Circuit." A pre-October 1981 Fifth Circuit decision is Eleventh Circuit precedent. (**UNRESOLVED**: whether *Bonner* carries pre-1981 *unpublished* old-Fifth decisions, which 5th Cir. R. 47.5.3 makes precedent in the Fifth.)

**Federal Circuit.** *South Corp. v. United States*, 690 F.2d 1368, 1369 (Fed. Cir. 1982) (en banc): the holdings of the Court of Claims and the Court of Customs and Patent Appeals announced before the close of business September 30, 1982 "shall be binding as precedent in this court."

### One caution on the federal baseline

The broad formulation — *any* vacated opinion is a nullity — is circuit gloss (*Durning*), not a Supreme Court holding. Both Supreme Court statements are framed as consequences of that Court's own disposition ("leaving this Court's opinion and judgment as the sole law of the case"). And a court can vacate the judgment while expressly preserving the opinion: D.C. Cir. R. 40(d) does exactly that as its default. "No precedential authority" is also not "may not be cited" — vacated opinions remain citable for their reasoning, procedural history, and preclusion.

## Fractured, plurality, and equally divided decisions

### The Marks rule

***Marks v. United States***, 430 U.S. 188, 193 (1977): "When a fragmented Court decides a case and no single rationale explaining the result enjoys the assent of five Justices, 'the holding of the Court may be viewed as that position taken by those Members who concurred in the judgments on the narrowest grounds . . . .' *Gregg v. Georgia*, 428 U.S. 153, 169 n.15 (1976)."

Two drafting notes: only the second half is a quotation, and it is "judgments," plural.

### Where Marks breaks

*Nichols v. United States*, 511 U.S. 738, 745–46 (1994): "We think it not useful to pursue the *Marks* inquiry to the utmost logical possibility when it has so obviously baffled and divided the lower courts that have considered it."

*King v. Palmer*, 950 F.2d 771 (D.C. Cir. 1991) (en banc), at 781: "*Marks* is workable — one opinion can be meaningfully regarded as 'narrower' than another — **only when one opinion is a logical subset of other, broader opinions.**" At 782: "If applied in situations where the various opinions supporting the judgment are mutually exclusive, *Marks* will turn a single opinion that lacks majority support into national law." At 783: "we do not think we are free to combine a dissent with a concurrence to form a *Marks* majority." At 784: "if the application of *Marks* will not yield a majority holding, nothing will."

*United States v. Duvall*, 740 F.3d 604 (D.C. Cir. 2013) (order denying rehearing en banc): Kavanaugh, J., concurring, at 618 ("*Marks* can sometimes seem like a Rubik's Cube") and at 610 (follow "the opinion that occupies the middle ground"); Williams, Sr. J., concurring, at 621–22 ("**some Supreme Court decisions yield no binding precedent**"). The Court granted certiorari on the *Marks* question in *Hughes v. United States*, 584 U.S. 675 (2018), and then declined to answer it.

### Equally divided courts

*Neil v. Biggers*, 409 U.S. 188, 192 (1972): "**Nor is an affirmance by an equally divided Court entitled to precedential weight.**"

*Durant v. Essex Co.*, 74 U.S. (7 Wall.) 107, 113 (1868): the "divided court" notation "serves to explain the absence of any opinion in the cause, and **prevents the decision from becoming an authority for other cases of like character. But the judgment is as conclusive and binding in every respect upon the parties** as if rendered upon the concurrence of all the judges."

State analogues in this file: Colo. C.A.R. 35(b); Iowa Code § 602.5106(1); Ind. App. R. 58(C) (even division after transfer granted **reinstates** the Court of Appeals decision); Mass. R. App. P. 27.1(g) (even division after FAR granted has the same effect as a denial). Wisconsin's rule on the precedential effect of an equally divided affirmance is **UNRESOLVED** (mechanic confirmed at *New Richmond News v. City of New Richmond*, 2015 WI 106 ¶ 1; no holding located).

## Tooling cautions

- **CourtListener's `precedential_status` is unreliable** in Mississippi (legacy "not designated" opinions tagged `Published`), Nebraska (memorandum opinions tagged `Published`), and Montana (N-suffix cases tagged both ways). Verify against the opinion's own caption.
- **DingDuff gaps and defects:** no California Rules of Court 8.1100–8.1125; no Oregon, Rhode Island, South Carolina, Vermont, West Virginia, or Wyoming court rules; Oklahoma `OK-SCAP`/`OK-CRAP` are title-only stubs; Nevada's NRAP predates the Aug. 15, 2024 amendments; Wisconsin's ch. 809 section boundaries are off by one (§ 809.23 text sits in the `WI/809/809.22` record); identifiers containing spaces or "§" (Ariz. `ARSC 111`, Ky. `RAP 41`, Mo. `art. V, § 10`) are rejected by `codes_view` and `fetch_statute_file`.
- A **reporter citation is not proof of precedential status**: Montana N-suffix opinions, Oklahoma "released for publication" COCA opinions, Alabama two-judge Civil Appeals opinions, and reported Pennsylvania Commonwealth Court single-judge opinions all carry full reporter cites and are not binding precedent.
